# The Care Notebook Template Catalog

A free, editable set of starting points for anyone keeping track of another person's care.

Built for the United States. Written in plain language. Free to copy, translate, and change.

---

## How to use this

Every template here is a **starting point, not a rule**. Care never goes the way a form expects. Take what helps, delete the rest, and add whatever your situation actually needs.

Nothing in this catalog is medical or legal advice. It does not tell you what care someone should get or what your rights are in your state. It helps you write down what happened, who you talked to, and what you were told.

**A note about where you live.** Some of these processes are the same everywhere in the country. Others work completely differently from one state to the next. Where that is true, the template says so.

**License.** CC BY-SA 4.0. Use it, change it, share it, including commercially. Keep the same license on what you share and credit the source.

# Part one: care settings

Pick the one closest to your situation. Each sets up who to write down, what is running at the same time, and what to do in the first days.

## Nursing home

*Long term stay in a skilled nursing facility*

**What makes this hard.** The long haul. Care plan meetings, grievances, billing surprises, and watching for any talk of discharge.

**People to write down**

- Director of nursing
- Charge nurse on the unit
- Social worker or family liaison
- Assessment coordinator
- Administrator
- Business office or billing

**Running at the same time**

- Nursing
- Daily personal care
- Activities
- Meals and dietary
- Social services

**First days checklist**

1. Write down the direct phone number for the unit, not just the main line
2. Ask for the current medication list and keep a copy
3. Ask who calls you when something changes, and how they reach you at night
4. Write down the room number, floor, and wing
5. Meet the assigned social worker and save the direct number
6. Ask for the written grievance procedure
7. Save the long term care ombudsman contact for your area
8. Ask when care plan meetings happen and ask to be included
9. Ask the business office what is covered and what is billed separately
10. Ask what the facility does with personal laundry and labeled belongings

**Papers worth having a photo of**

- Admission agreement
- Resident rights notice
- Current medication list
- Care plan summary
- Insurance and coverage cards
- Advance directive or medical orders

**Keep in front:** Standing instructions, The trail, Care threads, Money  
**Tuck away until needed:** Appointments, Progress

# Part two: the long processes

Applications, appeals, and paperwork fights. These run for months and involve people who do not talk to each other. Each one below is a sequence you can work through and check off.

## Medicaid application for long term care

*Applying for coverage of nursing home or facility care*

> Details vary by state. Treat this as the general shape, not the rule where you live.

**How long this usually takes.** Often a few months. Longer whenever a document is missing.

**Who is involved**

- Assigned caseworker
- Local Medicaid office
- Facility business office
- Elder law attorney, if you use one
- Bank contact for statements

**Steps**

1. Find out which program you are applying to and get the right form
2. Write down the local office address, phone, and hours
3. Gather identification and proof of where they live
4. Gather proof of all income
5. Gather bank statements going back as far as the state asks
6. Gather property, insurance, and burial arrangement papers
7. Write down anything that was sold, given away, or transferred, and when
8. Submit the application and note the date you submitted it
9. Get a case number and write it down
10. Get the assigned caseworker's name and direct number
11. Answer every request for more documents and log the date you sent each one
12. Ask what the decision deadline is and write it down
13. If it is denied, write down the appeal deadline immediately
14. Keep a copy of everything you send and everything you receive

**Papers to gather**

- Application form
- Identification and proof of residence
- Income statements
- Bank statements
- Property and insurance papers
- Receipts for anything spent down
- Denial or approval letter

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on a caseworker to be assigned
- Waiting on a document request to be satisfied
- Waiting on a decision
- Waiting on a bank for older statements

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- One missing bank statement stalls everything
- Transfers nobody remembers making
- A document request that arrives while you are away

## Medicaid waiver for care at home

*Applying for services that let someone stay out of a facility*

> Details vary by state. Treat this as the general shape, not the rule where you live.

**How long this usually takes.** The application takes weeks to months. The waiting list can take much longer, sometimes years in some states.

**Who is involved**

- Waiver caseworker
- Area agency on aging
- Person who does the needs assessment
- Managed care contact, if there is one
- Elder law attorney, if you use one

**Steps**

1. Find out which waiver programs exist where you live
2. Ask which one fits the situation and write down the name
3. Ask what the needs assessment involves and who does it
4. Ask what the financial requirements are
5. Gather the documents they ask for
6. Find the local contact and write down the phone and address
7. Submit the application and note the date
8. Ask directly whether there is a waiting list
9. If there is a list, get your placement date in writing and ask about priority categories
10. Get a case number and the caseworker's direct number
11. Ask how you will be notified when a spot opens
12. Answer every document request and log the date
13. Ask how often you need to confirm you are still waiting
14. Note the reassessment schedule once services start

**Papers to gather**

- Application form
- Needs assessment results
- Income and asset documents
- Waiting list placement letter
- Notice that a spot is available
- Service plan once approved

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on the needs assessment to be scheduled
- Waiting on a waiting list placement letter
- Waiting on a spot to open
- Waiting on a caseworker to return a call

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- Sitting on a waiting list with no updates for months
- Not knowing whether you are still on the list
- Missing the notice when a spot finally opens

## Appeal when Medicare coverage is ending

*Challenging a discharge or the end of covered services*

**How long this usually takes.** Very fast. Deadlines are often measured in hours, and decisions come within a day or two.

**Who is involved**

- Your regional Medicare quality review organization
- Case manager at the hospital or facility
- Treating doctor
- Free state insurance counseling program

**Steps**

1. Find the written notice and read it all the way through
2. Write down which notice it is and the date you received it
3. Write down the deadline on the notice, exactly as it appears
4. Call the review organization listed on the notice before the deadline
5. Ask for the detailed notice that explains the reason
6. Ask the treating doctor whether they will support continued care in writing
7. Gather any records that show what care is still needed
8. Write down that coverage does not require improvement, only a need for skilled care
9. Note the date and time of every call and who you spoke with
10. Write down the reference number for the appeal
11. Ask when a decision will come and how it will arrive
12. If it is denied, ask what the next level of appeal is and the deadline
13. Keep copies of every notice and every submission

**Papers to gather**

- The notice you received
- The detailed notice explaining the reason
- Supporting records
- A letter from the doctor if you got one
- Appeal confirmation and reference number
- Decision letter

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on the detailed notice
- Waiting on a letter from the doctor
- Waiting on the review decision

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- The deadline is often within a day, so a weekend can cost the appeal
- Being told coverage ends because there is no improvement, which is not the standard
- Not asking for the detailed notice

## Appeal an insurance denial

*When insurance refuses to approve or pay for something*

> Details vary by state. Treat this as the general shape, not the rule where you live.

**How long this usually takes.** Usually weeks. Faster if the situation is urgent and you say so.

**Who is involved**

- Insurance member services
- Insurance appeals department
- Office of the doctor who ordered it
- Employer benefits contact, if it is a work plan
- State insurance department

**Steps**

1. Get the denial letter and find the stated reason
2. Write down the appeal deadline
3. Call member services and ask them to explain the process step by step
4. Ask for the written criteria they used to decide
5. Ask the ordering doctor's office to write a letter explaining why it is needed
6. Gather supporting records
7. Submit the appeal in writing and keep proof of sending
8. Get a reference number
9. Log every call with the date, time, and name
10. Ask when the decision is due
11. If it is denied again, ask about an outside review and the deadline
12. Keep copies of everything

**Papers to gather**

- Denial letter
- The plan's written criteria
- Letter from the doctor
- Supporting records
- Appeal submission and reference number
- Decision letter

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on the doctor's letter
- Waiting on the plan's criteria
- Waiting on the appeal decision

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- Missing the appeal window
- Never getting the letter from the doctor
- Stopping after the first denial instead of asking for outside review

## Planning a discharge home

*Getting ready before someone leaves the hospital or facility*

**How long this usually takes.** Compressed. You often get a day or two of notice.

**Who is involved**

- Discharge planner or case manager
- Social worker
- Doctor in charge
- Home health or equipment coordinator
- Your regional Medicare quality review organization

**Steps**

1. Write down who to call with questions after discharge
2. Ask where care happens next and what the options are
3. Ask what equipment is needed and who orders and delivers it
4. Ask what help will be needed with daily activities, and be honest about what you can do
5. Ask for a demonstration of anything you will have to do yourself
6. Ask the social worker about cost and coverage questions
7. Ask for the written discharge instructions before you leave
8. Ask for a written summary of the current condition and medications
9. Check the medication list against what they were taking before
10. Write down every follow up appointment and who makes them
11. Ask what problems to watch for and exactly who to call about each
12. Write down who to contact if the discharge feels too soon

**Papers to gather**

- Written discharge instructions
- Summary of condition and medications
- Updated medication list
- Equipment orders
- Follow up appointment list

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on equipment delivery
- Waiting on home health to be arranged
- Waiting on the written instructions
- Waiting on a follow up appointment to be scheduled

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- Discharge happens before the equipment arrives
- Nobody says who makes the follow up appointments
- The medication list changed and nobody explained why

## Requesting medical records

*Getting copies of records you have a right to see*

**Worth knowing.** Getting a copy of medical records is a federal right under health privacy law, and fees are limited to the cost of copying.

**How long this usually takes.** Federal rules give them about a month, with one extension allowed if they tell you in writing.

**Who is involved**

- Medical records department
- Facility administrator, if you get stuck
- Federal health privacy office, for complaints

**Steps**

1. Figure out exactly who holds the records you want
2. Ask for their records request form and their process
3. Confirm what proof they need that you are authorized to request
4. Write the request down, naming the dates and the records you want
5. Say what format you want, paper or electronic
6. Submit it and write down the date you submitted
7. Ask what the deadline is for them to respond
8. Ask what the fee is and ask for it in writing
9. Follow up in writing if the deadline passes
10. Note you do not have to explain why you want them
11. If they will not respond, write down the complaint option and the date you filed
12. Keep a copy of the request, the responses, and everything you receive

**Papers to gather**

- Your written request
- Proof you are authorized
- Fee statement
- The records you receive
- Any denial letter
- Complaint confirmation

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on the response deadline
- Waiting on a fee estimate
- Waiting on records after paying
- Waiting on a complaint response

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- Being told a patient portal is the only option
- Being charged more than copying actually costs
- The deadline passing with no answer and no follow up in writing

## Filing a complaint about a facility

*Going to the ombudsman or the state agency*

**How long this usually takes.** Urgent complaints move in days. Others can wait weeks or until the next inspection.

**Who is involved**

- Long term care ombudsman
- State agency that inspects facilities
- Adult protective services, for suspected abuse
- Facility administrator

**Steps**

1. Write down what happened with dates, times, and the names of everyone involved
2. Decide which route you want: the ombudsman helps resolve, the state agency inspects and enforces
3. Find the ombudsman contact for your area
4. Find the state agency contact
5. Gather your notes, photos, and any records you already have
6. Ask whether you can file without your name being shared
7. File the complaint and write down the date
8. Get a reference number
9. Ask what priority it was given and what that means for timing
10. Ask when to expect contact and from whom
11. Follow up on the date they gave you
12. Write down the outcome and anything the facility was told to fix

**Papers to gather**

- Your written account of what happened
- Photos, if any
- Related records
- Complaint confirmation and reference number
- Any written outcome

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on a return call from the ombudsman
- Waiting on an investigation to be scheduled
- Waiting on findings
- Waiting on the facility's correction plan

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- Losing the reference number
- A lower priority complaint sitting until the next scheduled inspection
- Not writing down names on the day it happened

## Applying for disability benefits

*A long application with a lot of medical history*

**How long this usually takes.** Months for a first decision. Appeals add more months.

**Who is involved**

- Local Social Security office
- Disability advocate or attorney, if you use one
- Doctors who will provide records
- Two people who know the situation well

**Steps**

1. Find out which program applies and get the starter materials
2. Gather identification and the social security number
3. List every doctor, clinic, and hospital with dates of treatment
4. List work history going back as far as they ask
5. Build the current medication list
6. Write down two people who can describe the daily situation
7. Complete the application, online or in person
8. Submit and write down the date and the claim number
9. Answer every request for more information and log the dates
10. Ask when a decision is expected
11. If it is denied, write down the appeal deadline the same day
12. Keep copies of everything, including the denial

**Papers to gather**

- Identification and social security number
- List of doctors and treatment dates
- Work history
- Medication list
- Application confirmation
- Decision or denial letter

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on medical records to be sent
- Waiting on a decision
- Waiting on an appeal hearing date

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- Gaps in the medical history
- Missing an appeal deadline after a denial
- Incomplete work history

## Guardianship or conservatorship

*A court process for decision making when someone cannot decide*

> Details vary by state. Treat this as the general shape, not the rule where you live.

**Careful here.** This is not legal advice. Guardianship law is different in every state and a lawyer is usually involved.

**How long this usually takes.** Weeks to months. Much longer if anyone contests it.

**Who is involved**

- Probate or family court clerk
- Attorney
- Court appointed evaluator or examining doctor
- Court investigator

**Steps**

1. Understand that this is a court process and it works differently in every state
2. Ask whether a less restrictive option would work instead, like a power of attorney
3. Find the court's self help resource or information line
4. Find out which forms are required and where to file
5. Find out whether an attorney is required where you live
6. Gather the medical documentation the court requires
7. Find out who has to be notified and how
8. File the petition and write down the case number
9. Write down the hearing date and what to bring
10. Attend the hearing
11. Get a copy of the court order
12. Write down what reports you must file afterward and when they are due

**Papers to gather**

- Petition and court forms
- Medical documentation
- Notices sent to family
- Court order
- Ongoing reports you have to file

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on an attorney consultation
- Waiting on medical documentation
- Waiting on a hearing date
- Waiting on the signed order

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- Starting a court case when a simpler document would have worked
- Missing the reports that are due after appointment
- Not knowing who must be notified

## Setting up legal and medical decision papers

*Power of attorney, health care decision maker, and written wishes*

> Details vary by state. Treat this as the general shape, not the rule where you live.

**Careful here.** This is not legal advice. What these documents are called and how they must be signed varies by state.

**How long this usually takes.** Can be done in a week. Signed medical orders need a doctor.

**Who is involved**

- Attorney, if you use one
- Notary
- Primary doctor, for medical orders
- The person being named to decide

**Steps**

1. Learn which documents exist and what each one does
2. Note that money decisions and medical decisions are usually separate documents
3. Note that written wishes are planning, while signed medical orders are followed by responders
4. Decide who will be named, and name a backup
5. Get the forms that are valid in your state
6. Complete them while the person can still make their own decisions
7. Sign with whatever witnesses or notary your state requires
8. Give copies to the named person, the doctors, and the facility
9. Write down where every original is kept, specifically
10. Ask the doctor about signed medical orders if the situation is serious
11. Put all of it on the emergency card
12. Review after any major change in health

**Papers to gather**

- Money decision document
- Health care decision document
- Written wishes
- Signed medical orders, if applicable
- A note of where each original is kept

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on an attorney appointment
- Waiting on a notary
- Waiting on the doctor to sign medical orders
- Waiting on copies to reach the facility

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- Signed incorrectly for your state and not valid
- Nobody has a copy when it is needed
- Confusing written wishes with orders that responders can follow

## Taking leave from work to provide care

*Requesting protected time off for yourself*

**How long this usually takes.** Days to a few weeks, mostly waiting on forms.

**Who is involved**

- Human resources at your employer
- The doctor who will complete the form
- Federal labor department, if there is a problem

**Steps**

1. Check whether your employer and your own work history qualify
2. Tell your employer you need time off to care for a family member
3. Give as much notice as you can, in writing
4. Ask human resources for the paperwork
5. Get the medical certification form to the doctor's office quickly
6. Note the deadline for returning the certification
7. Keep the notices your employer gives you
8. Ask whether the leave is paid, unpaid, or can be combined with paid time
9. Ask how the leave gets tracked if you take it a few hours at a time
10. Keep your own log of every hour used
11. Confirm in writing what was approved and for how long
12. Ask what happens when the leave runs out

**Papers to gather**

- Your written request
- Medical certification form
- Employer notices
- Your own hours log
- Written approval

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on the paperwork from human resources
- Waiting on the doctor to complete the form
- Waiting on written approval

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- The certification arrives late and the leave is denied
- Nobody tracks the hours taken a little at a time
- Assuming it is paid when it is not

## Moving to a different facility

*Arranging a transfer from one place to another*

> Details vary by state. Treat this as the general shape, not the rule where you live.

**How long this usually takes.** Days to weeks, depending on beds and approvals.

**Who is involved**

- Case manager where they are now
- Admissions at the new place
- Insurance or coverage contact
- Transport service

1. Write down the reason for the move and what you want instead
2. Confirm the new place has a bed and accepts the coverage

11. Rebuild the contact list for the new place
12. Keep copies of the transfer paperwork

- Coverage authorization
- Any bed hold notice
- New contact list

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on a bed to open

- Waiting on records to transfer
- Waiting on a transport time

- Records and medication lists that never arrive
- Losing the old bed before the new one is confirmed
- A coverage gap between the two places

## Fighting a facility discharge

*When a facility says someone has to leave*

> Details vary by state. Treat this as the general shape, not the rule where you live.

**Worth knowing.** Federal rules for nursing homes limit why a facility can discharge someone, generally require advance written notice, and give a right to appeal.

**Careful here.** This is not legal advice. Call the ombudsman early, they do this for free.

**How long this usually takes.** Fast. The appeal usually has to be filed before the discharge date on the notice.

**Who is involved**

- Long term care ombudsman
- State office that handles these appeals
- Facility administrator
- Legal aid or attorney

**Steps**

1. Get the notice in writing and read it carefully
2. Check that it states a reason, where they are supposed to go, and how to appeal
3. Write down the date on the notice and the date you received it
4. Note that federal rules allow facility initiated discharge only for a short list of reasons
5. Note that a notice of only a day or two is almost never proper
6. Call the ombudsman the same day
7. File the appeal by the deadline on the notice
8. Ask whether they can stay while the appeal is decided
9. Gather payment records and care records
10. Write down every conversation with the facility, with names and times
11. Prepare for the hearing and ask the ombudsman what to expect
12. If they are in the hospital, ask about the right to come back to their bed
13. Keep copies of everything

**Papers to gather**

- The discharge notice
- Your appeal request
- Payment records
- Care records
- Hearing materials
- Any bed hold notice

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on a call back from the ombudsman
- Waiting on a hearing date
- Waiting on written confirmation they can stay
- Waiting on the decision

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- Very short notice that nobody challenges
- Confusing coverage ending with being evicted, they are different things
- Being refused readmission after a hospital stay

## Documenting money for a Medicaid application

*Organizing years of financial records*

> Details vary by state. Treat this as the general shape, not the rule where you live.

**Careful here.** This is not legal or financial advice. Rules vary by state and mistakes here have real consequences.

**How long this usually takes.** Runs alongside the application. Gathering old statements is usually the slow part.

**Who is involved**

- Medicaid caseworker
- Elder law attorney or Medicaid planner
- Bank
- Facility business office

**Steps**

1. Ask exactly how many years of records the state wants
2. Request statements from every bank and account for that whole period
3. List every account, property, policy, and vehicle
4. Write down anything that was sold, gifted, or moved, with dates
5. Keep receipts for medical bills that were paid
6. Keep receipts for home changes and repairs
7. Keep any prepaid funeral or burial paperwork
8. Organize everything by year and by account
9. Ask the caseworker what counts and what does not where you live
10. Consider a consultation with someone who does this professionally
11. Submit the documentation together, not piece by piece
12. Keep a copy of everything you handed over

**Papers to gather**

- Bank statements for the full lookback period
- Property and vehicle records
- Insurance policies
- Documentation of transfers or gifts
- Medical bill receipts
- Home repair receipts
- Burial or funeral arrangements

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on older bank statements
- Waiting on a professional consultation
- Waiting on the caseworker to confirm what is missing

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- A transfer nobody can explain, which can cause a penalty period
- Statements the bank charges for or takes weeks to produce
- Assuming something is exempt when it is not

## Sorting out bills and insurance statements

*Matching what was billed against what was covered*

**How long this usually takes.** Ongoing. Individual disputes take weeks.

**Who is involved**

- Billing office where the care happened
- Insurance member services
- Free state insurance counseling program
- State insurance department

**Steps**

1. Collect every bill and every insurance statement in one place
2. Match each bill to the statement that covers the same visit
3. Write down what insurance paid and what is actually owed
4. Flag anything where the two do not match
5. Do not pay a bill before the insurance statement arrives
6. Call the billing office about anything unclear and log the call
7. Call insurance if the statement looks wrong
8. Ask for a corrected statement or bill in writing
9. Open a dispute if it is not resolved and get a reference number
10. Write down the payment deadline so a dispute does not become a late payment
11. Ask whether a payment plan is available if the balance is real
12. Keep copies of everything

**Papers to gather**

- Bills
- Insurance statements
- Corrected statements
- Dispute correspondence and reference numbers
- Payment records

**What you end up waiting on**

- Waiting on an insurance statement
- Waiting on a corrected bill
- Waiting on a dispute decision

**Where this usually goes wrong**

- Paying before the statement arrives
- A disputed bill going to collections while you wait
- Bills from providers you never chose

# Part three: things to measure

Only track what someone actually asked you to track, or what you personally want to see over time. Everything here records numbers and observations. Nothing here tells you whether a number is good or bad. That is a conversation for a clinician.

## Weight

- **Units:** lb, kg
- **How often:** Weekly, or whenever a clinician asks
- **What you write down:** value, date, note
- **Worth marking medication starts on this one**
- **Note:** Medication start markers are useful here, for example when a new medication is started and the family wants the timing visible on the chart.

## Blood pressure

- **Units:** mmHg
- **How often:** As a clinician asks
- **What you write down:** top number, bottom number, date, who took it, note
- **Worth marking medication starts on this one**
- **Note:** Record who took the reading, since readings taken at home and at a clinic often differ.

## Blood sugar

- **Units:** mg/dL, mmol/L
- **How often:** As a clinician asks
- **What you write down:** value, date, time, before or after eating, note
- **Worth marking medication starts on this one**

## Moving around

- **Units:** feet, meters, steps, minutes
- **How often:** Per session, or weekly
- **What you write down:** distance or time, help needed, date, note
- **Choices:** No help, A little help, A lot of help, Could not today
- **Note:** Plain language on purpose. Record what the therapist said, not your own rating.

## Wound or pressure sore

- **Units:** no units, just notes
- **How often:** Per dressing change or clinician visit
- **What you write down:** photo, date, location on body, what the clinician said, note
- **Note:** Photographs and dates only. The stage field records what a clinician told you, and the label must say so.

## Pain

- **Units:** 0 to 10
- **How often:** As needed
- **What you write down:** level, date, where, note
- **Worth marking medication starts on this one**
- **Note:** This is their own report of their pain, not your assessment of it.

## Sleep

- **Units:** hours
- **How often:** Daily
- **What you write down:** hours, date, times woke up, note
- **Worth marking medication starts on this one**

## Eating

- **Units:** no units, just notes
- **How often:** Per meal or daily
- **What you write down:** how much, date, meal, note
- **Choices:** Ate most of it, Ate about half, Ate a little, Did not eat
- **Worth marking medication starts on this one**

## Mood and behavior

- **Units:** no units, just notes
- **How often:** Daily or when something happens
- **What you write down:** what you saw, date, time of day, who reported it, note
- **Worth marking medication starts on this one**
- **Note:** Observation only. The field is what you saw or what staff told you, never a conclusion about why.

## Fluids

- **Units:** mL, oz, cups
- **How often:** Only when a clinician asks the family to track it
- **What you write down:** amount, date, in or out, note

## Dialysis sessions

- **Units:** hours, minutes
- **How often:** Every session
- **What you write down:** date, attended or missed, length, how they felt after, note
- **Note:** Attendance and their own report of how they felt. Nothing clinical.

## Treatment cycles

- **Units:** no units, just notes
- **How often:** Per cycle
- **What you write down:** cycle number, date, what you noticed after, note
- **Worth marking medication starts on this one**
- **Note:** Side effects are recorded as observations of what happened, not as something to act on.

## Falls

- **Units:** no units, just notes
- **How often:** Every time it happens
- **What you write down:** date, time, where it happened, what happened, what was done, who told you, who you notified, note
- **Note:** This connects to standing instructions about being notified after a fall. Record who told you and when.

## Growth

- **Units:** in, cm, lb, kg
- **How often:** At each visit
- **What you write down:** height, weight, head measurement, date, note
- **Note:** Record the numbers the clinic measured. Never show percentiles or comparisons.

## Temperature

- **Units:** F, C
- **How often:** As needed
- **What you write down:** value, date, time, note

## Bathroom

- **Units:** no units, just notes
- **How often:** Daily, if it matters right now
- **What you write down:** date, note
- **Note:** Kept deliberately plain and private. Some families track this during a specific problem and then stop.

# Part four: standing instructions

These are the rules you give a facility or an agency. Write down what you asked for, who you asked, when, and what they said back. Then you have a record instead of a memory.

**Backed by federal rules for nursing homes.** Federal rules for nursing homes that take Medicare or Medicaid require this. Other kinds of places, like assisted living and home care agencies, are not covered by those same rules.

**Your request.** This is a reasonable thing to ask for, but no rule requires them to agree. Ask for it in writing anyway.

## Call me about any fall or injury

> Please call me right away if there is a fall or any injury, and tell me what happened and what was done.

**Backed by federal rules for nursing homes**

Federal nursing home rules require the facility to notify the resident's representative about an accident with an injury that may need a doctor's attention, and about significant changes in condition.

**What to ask for.** Ask for it to be written into the care plan, and ask who makes the call at night.

---

## Tell me about medication changes

> Please tell me when any medication is started, stopped, or changed.

**Backed by federal rules for nursing homes**

Federal nursing home rules require notice when there is a need to change treatment significantly. Small routine changes may not be covered, so ask for those too.

**What to ask for.** Ask for it in the care plan, and ask who will call.

---

## Tell me before treatment changes

> Please tell me before starting or stopping any treatment or therapy.

**Backed by federal rules for nursing homes**

Federal nursing home rules require notice of a significant change in treatment.

**What to ask for.** Ask for it in the care plan.

---

## Include me in care plan meetings

> Please include me in every care plan meeting and tell me the schedule ahead of time.

**Backed by federal rules for nursing homes**

Federal nursing home rules give residents and their representatives the right to take part in care planning.

**What to ask for.** Ask for the meeting schedule in writing and ask to be added to the notification list.

---

## Call me immediately about any hospital transfer

> If they are sent to the hospital, please call me right away and tell me where and why.

**Backed by federal rules for nursing homes**

Federal nursing home rules require notice of a decision to transfer or discharge.

**What to ask for.** Ask what the after hours call process is, and get the name of who makes that call.

---

## Follow the signed orders and call me first

> Our wishes are in the signed orders dated [date], kept at [location]. Please follow them and call me before anything changes.

**Backed by federal rules for nursing homes**

Federal nursing home rules require the facility to honor advance directives and to tell you its policies about them.

**What to ask for.** Confirm the signed orders are in the chart and flagged where staff will see them fast.

---

## Who you can share information with

> Please share information with these people: [list]. Please follow these visitor preferences: [list].

**Backed by federal rules for nursing homes**

Federal nursing home rules protect visitation and give control over who receives information. Specific preferences beyond that are requests.

**What to ask for.** Confirm the authorized contact list is on file and ask to see it.

---

## Tell me before a room or roommate change

> Please tell me before any room change or roommate change.

**Your request**

There are notice rules around moves within a facility, but being told in advance about a roommate change is mostly something you have to ask for.

**What to ask for.** Ask for it to be added to the care plan so it survives staff turnover.

---

## Get my okay before outside services that cost money

> Please get my okay before sending them to any outside dentist, podiatrist, lab, or other service that may be billed to us.

**Your request**

No rule requires a facility to get your approval first, though there are separate rules about cost estimates and out of network notices in some situations.

**What to ask for.** Ask for it in writing in the file, and ask for a cost estimate before anything is scheduled.

---

## Do not discuss discharge without me

> Please do not discuss discharge or moving without me present or informed first.

**Your request**

You do have a right to be notified of a decision to transfer or discharge, and a right to appeal it. Being present for every conversation is something you have to ask for.

**What to ask for.** Ask for the notice process in writing, and ask who is responsible for calling you.

---

## Keep these food restrictions on file

> Please keep these food restrictions and preferences on file: [list]. Tell me if they cannot be followed.

**Your request**

Care plans are supposed to reflect needs and preferences, so this is well supported, but the specific list is something you provide and confirm.

**What to ask for.** Confirm it is in both the care plan and the kitchen's system, and check back after a month.
